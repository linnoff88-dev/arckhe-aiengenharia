ðŸ”„ Think in systems, not commands

From a one-off request to a workflow that works without relying on your memory. You learn to see AI as one part of a process â€” not a standalone oracle â€” and to sketch that process on paper.

What is:

A workflow is a sequence of steps where the output of one becomes the input of the next. Instead of making isolated requests, you map out what goes in, what the AI does, what comes out, how you evaluate it, and what you adjust.

Why learn:

With a designed workflow, anyone (or you tomorrow) can carry out the entire process without relying on memory. The work starts flowing instead of getting stuck.

Key concepts:

Workflow, linked steps, input, output, memory dependency eliminated.

What is:

AI can take on different roles within a workflow: at one point it interprets raw input; next it generates a proposal; then it evaluates its own output against criteria you defined. Each role is a box in your design.

Why learn:

Understanding the possible roles helps you avoid asking for everything at once in one giant prompt. A well-designed system distributes responsibilities across manageable steps.

Key concepts:

AI roles, process box, interpretation, generation, evaluation, partial execution.

What is:

The exercise of taking a real process (creating a post, writing an analysis, putting together a lesson) and drawing the boxes on paper: what goes in, what each box does, and what comes out of each one.

Why learn:

Writing it down reveals steps you were doing mentally without realizing itâ€”and bottlenecks that were previously invisible. The diagram is the first version of the system.

Key concepts:

Workflow diagrammed, process boxes, visual map, bottleneck revealed.

What is:

Not every process is linear. A decision point is where the workflow can take different paths based on a criterion: â€œapproved â†’ deliver; rejected â†’ revise.â€ These points are drawn as diamonds or branches.

Why learn:

Mapping the branching paths turns the implicit logic in your head into an explicit system rule â€” any executor (human or AI) knows what to do in each case.

Key concepts:

Decision point, condition, branch, explicit criterion, system logic.

What is:

Every system has a handoff: the moment when AI passes the baton to a human (approve, adjust, publish, decide) and vice versa. Explicitly marking these points is what distinguishes a real workflow from an illusion of automation.

Why learn:

Knowing where humans are irreplaceable helps you avoid two opposite mistakes: automating what requires human judgment and manually doing what AI could do in seconds.

Key concepts:

Handoff, human approval point, accountability, judgment vs. execution.

What is:

The fundamental cycle of any intelligent system: you define criteria (Think), AI acts (Act), the result is checked against the criteria (Evaluate), anything that falls short is adjusted (Adjust), and the cycle starts again (Act).

Why learn:

Without the loop, the system runs once and stops. With the loop, the system improves on its own until it meets the criteriaâ€”which is the essence of intelligent work.

Key concepts:
