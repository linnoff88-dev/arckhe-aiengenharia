🎯 Task × Intent

In this module, you’ll learn the difference between what you ask for and what you want to achieve—and how to state your intent so AI gets to the right place.

🔀 The difference between what I ask for and what I want to achieve

Task and intent seem like the same thing until you see the post that sold nothing. The prompt asked for "a text about the product" — and the AI delivered exactly that. Polished, well-structured, correct text. No sales at all. The problem wasn’t the prompt: it was that the real intention was never stated.

✗ Without stating intent

  • ✗AI chooses the destination on its own
  • ✗Correct answer to a goal you didn’t have
  • ✗You judge the text by how it looks, not by the result

✓ With stated intent

  • ✓AI adjusts tone, structure, and call to action based on the destination
  • ✓You judge by the effect: “will it work?”—not “is it correct?”
  • ✓Rework decreases because the direction was right from the start

Task

The verb in the request—“write,” “summarize,” “analyze”

Intent

The effect the result should have in the real world

Right evaluation

"Will it work?" not "is it right?"

The gap

AI gets the task right and can get the destination wrong

2

❓ The 6 questions about intent

Before typing any important request, 6 questions reveal the intent you haven’t articulated yet. Each answer changes how the AI will build the result. 2 minutes here save you 20 minutes of rework later.

1

What’s the end goal?

Not the task—the real-world outcome. “A written post” isn’t an objective. “200 clicks on the link in the bio” is.

2

What transformation do I want to bring about?

The reader starts at A (unaware, skeptical, or undecided) and needs to get to B (informed, trusting, and ready to decide). How much ground does the copy need to cover?

3

What decision will this enable?

Every result should enable a decision — buy, approve, schedule, share, change your mind. If there's no decision, why does the text exist?

4

Who will read/use this?

Not just “my audience”—the state of the person who will read it: are they convinced? Do they have questions? Are they comparing prices? Each state calls for different copy.

5

What emotional/practical effect do you want?

Emotional: confidence, urgency, curiosity, security. Practical: click, save, forward, fill out. State both—AI uses both to calibrate the text.

6

Why does this matter now?

Urgency and time relevance. "It’s enrollment season," "the product is gone tomorrow," "it’s Monday and time to go back to work"—time context changes the text’s tone and structure.

💡 Practical Tip

You don’t need to answer all 6 questions every time. Answer the ones you still don’t know — these are exactly the ones that were missing from your prompt. One more question answered can already change the response.

3

📄 The same post, 3 intentions — 3 completely different texts

The product: an online photography course for beginners. The task is identical in all three cases: "write a post about this course." What changes is the stated intent—and the three texts don't even seem to be about the same product.

I1

Intent 1 — Inform

End goal: increase organic reach and educate followers who don’t know me yet. Desired effect: the reader learns something useful and follows me. [post request]

What you get: "Did you know the rule of thirds can turn any mediocre photo into a good one?" — An educational post with a concrete tip and accessible language. It doesn’t mention a price or include a purchase CTA. End with "Save this post to use on your next outing."

I2

Intent 2 — Sell ← this was the real intent

End goal: get the reader to click the link in the bio and enroll in the course. Audience: followers who have been following me for more than 3 months and still haven’t bought. Effect: urgency + confidence to make the decision today. [post request]

What you get: "6 months ago, I was in your shoes — obsessed with taking better photos but not knowing where to start." — Post with a personal story, social proof, concrete course benefits, a time-limited offer, and a direct CTA: "Link in bio. Enrollment closes Friday."

I3

Intent 3 — Build Authority

Objective: be seen as Brazil’s #1 reference for beginner photography. Effect: the reader shares the post and recommends me to friends who want to learn. [post request]

What you get: "I taught photography to 1,200 students. This is the mistake 9 out of 10 make in their first week." — Positioning post, expert tone, data, list of common mistakes. No sales CTA. Ends with "If you know someone who wants to learn, forward this."

🔍 What the creator originally asked for—and why it went wrong

The original prompt was: "Write a text about the photography course." AI chose the default intent—and chose to inform, because that’s the safest default when the intent isn’t stated.

The result? I1-style text—polished, educational, engaging. And zero sales. Because the real intent was I2. A better prompt wouldn’t solve this. Only a stated intention will.

4

💸 The cost of giving a good answer to the wrong target

Technical quality without the right intent isn’t neutral— is a liability. A brilliant text with the wrong intent creates an expectation you won't meet, positions you in a way that undermines your real goal, and uses the reader's attention without producing the result you needed.

HOW THE DAMAGE HAPPENS — WHAT HAPPENED TO THE POST’S CREATOR

1.

The post was published. It was beautiful. It got likes, complimentary comments, and saves.

2.

The creator was satisfied—"it worked." They didn’t realize the right metric was link clicks, not likes.

3.

The reader learned something useful, said thanks, and left. Without clicking, buying, or even noticing that something was for sale.

✗

The educational text trained readers to expect free tips—and made the purchase link feel like a strange surprise.

✗ Signs of the wrong intent

  • ✗Lots of praise, few concrete results
  • ✗You’re satisfied with vanity metrics (likes)
  • ✗Rework after seeing it didn’t work — without understanding why

✓ Evaluating against the right destination

  • ✓You define the success metric before requesting the text
  • ✓Measures "how many clicked?" not "how many liked it?"
  • ✓Understand why you got the result—and repeat or adjust it precisely

Pitfall

Polished text = false sense of success

Negative asset

Creates the wrong expectation for the reader

Real metric

The result, not the quality of the text

Solution

Define the metric before asking

5

📝 How to state your intention in the prompt

You already have the T1 Kit — prompt structure (role, task, context, format, example). The Kit T2 is the block that comes first: states where the result needs to end up before saying what to ask for. Together, the two give the AI a task and a destination — and let it calibrate every word.

KIT T2 — INTENT BLOCK (PASTE BEFORE THE PROMPT)

End goal: [the real effect I want to have on the world].
Audience: [who will read/use it + current state].
Effect/decision I want to generate: [action or transformation].
If you have to choose, prioritize: [clarity | persuasion | accuracy | speed].

--- (now the Kit T1 normally) ---
You are [ROLE]. Your task is [TASK]...

APPLIED EXAMPLE — THE POST THAT SOLD NOTHING, REWRITTEN

End goal: get the reader to click the link in the bio and enroll in the photography course.
Audience: followers who have been following me for 3+ months and still haven’t bought. They’re curious but undecided.
Effect: create enough urgency for the decision to happen today.
If you have to choose, prioritize: persuasion.

You are a launch copywriting specialist. Write an Instagram post
with a human, direct tone, based on a personal story, with social proof and a clear CTA.
Maximum 300 words. End with real urgency (a deadline or limited spots).

💡 Practical Tip

The “if you need to choose, prioritize” field is for when intent and clarity conflict. Persuasive copy sometimes needs to be emotional instead of logical. Precise copy sometimes needs to be direct instead of warm. State the priority—the AI will resolve the conflict for you.

6

🔬 When intent changes the entire response — 3 quick studies

The principle isn’t specific to marketing—it’s universal. Three cases in completely different fields where changing only the stated intent changes AI’s response from zero to one hundred.

📧 Case A — Delivery delay email

TASK WITHOUT INTENT

"Write an email explaining the delivery delay."

Result: informative, cold copy that describes the problem. Accurate. It doesn’t retain the customer.

WITH STATED INTENT

"Goal: make the client leave this interaction more satisfied than they were when they arrived — even with the delay. Effect: trust + desire to buy again."

Result: an empathetic email, with compensation offered and a personal tone. The customer replies, "thank you, I understand."

🍎 Case B — Lesson plan on fractions

TASK WITHOUT INTENT

"Create a lesson plan on fractions for 5th grade."

Result: a complete, academically sound plan. It doesn’t account for the fact that the exam is next week.

WITH STATED INTENT

"Goal: students should be able to solve fraction questions from the diagnostic assessment in 5 days. Focus: the question types that come up most often. Tone: practical, without extensive theory."

Result: a compact plan, exercises focused on the types of questions on the exam, timed daily review.

⚖️ Case C — Contract review

TASK WITHOUT INTENT

"Analyze the clauses in this service agreement."

Result: a summary of all clauses, in technical language. Complete, long, and unfocused on what matters.

WITH STATED INTENT

"Goal: my client needs to decide whether to sign or request a revision. Focus: point out only the clauses that create risk or put my client at a disadvantage. Format: list of risks with severity (high/medium/low)."

Result: 3 risks identified, severity classified, clear recommendation. The client decides in 5 minutes.

Universal

Works in any professional field

Standard

Always: task + intent → targeted result

Focus

Intent filters what’s relevant from what’s complete

Application

Identify your context — which of the 3 is yours?

✅ Intent checklist before you press enter

Concept learned — now it needs to become a habit. This 5-item checklist takes 30 seconds and prevents the most common mistake: delivering a technically correct result that’s no use to anyone.

INTENT CHECKLIST — RUN BEFORE ANY IMPORTANT REQUEST

Why item 5 is the most powerful

Most people only realize the intent was wrong afterward to receive the answer. Item 5 makes you anticipate the result before asking—mentally simulating what will come back and checking whether that's what you want.

If you imagine the AI’s response and think "yes, perfect," your intention is clear. If you think "hmm, it depends on how it writes," your intention isn’t clear. Go back and state it.

Trigger

Checklist = a habit that turns a concept into practice

Time

30 seconds — costs less than 1 rework cycle

Item 5

Simulate the result before you ask—the best filter

When to use

Any request with real consequences (work, client, public)
