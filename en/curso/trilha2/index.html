2.1 ~45 min

🎯 Task × Intent

The post that sold nothing case: perfect prompt, beautiful copy, no sales. The problem wasn’t the prompt—it was failing to say where the copy should lead. Here, you’ll learn the difference between what you ask for and what you want to achieve.

What is:

Task is the verb in the request—“write,” “summarize,” “list.” Intent is the effect that result should have in the real world: a sale, a decision, a change in behavior. AI can carry out the task brilliantly and still miss the intent.

Why learn:

Without this distinction, you judge the response by what it says—and you should judge it by what it will cause. This shift changes everything: from “Is it right?” to “Will it work?”

Key concepts:

Task, intent, destination, desired effect, evaluation by results.

What is:

Six questions that reveal your intention before you type a request: (1) What’s the end goal? (2) What change do I want to cause? (3) What decision will this enable? (4) Who will read/use it? (5) What emotional/practical effect do I want? (6) Why does this matter now?

Why learn:

Most people go straight to the request without answering these questions—and the AI's response reflects exactly that: right on the surface, wrong in its destination. 2 minutes of reflection save 20 minutes of rework.

Key concepts:

The 6 questions: final goal, desired transformation, real audience, expected effect.

What is:

The product: an online photography course. The task: “write a post about this course.” Three completely different intents produce three texts that don’t even seem to be about the same product: (1) inform—teach the reader about photography; (2) sell—get the reader to sign up; (3) authority—position the creator as an expert in the niche.

Why learn:

Seeing all three side by side makes it impossible to go back. You’ll never ask for “a text about X” again without stating what that text needs to accomplish.

Key concepts:

Informational intent, conversion intent, authority intent, effect by intent.

What is:

Brilliant writing with the wrong intent isn’t neutral—it’s a liability. It takes up space, consumes the reader’s attention, creates the wrong expectations, and can position you in a way that undermines your real goal. The example of the post that sold nothing: the writing was so "educational" that the reader learned, said thanks, and left without buying.

Why learn:

The technical quality trap: polished writing gives you the false impression that the task is done. Recognizing this bias is the first step toward evaluating results, not execution.

Key concepts:

Hidden cost, technical quality bias, results vs. execution, wrong target.

What is:

A 2–4-line structure you paste before any request: "Final goal: [...]. Audience: [...]. Effect/decision I want to drive: [...]." The AI uses this information to calibrate tone, structure, urgency, and call to action—without you having to specify every detail.

Why learn:

Stating your intent turns any average prompt into a focused one. It’s the difference between giving a map and giving an address. You already have Kit T1 (prompt structure)—the intent block is the GPS that tells you where to go.

Key concepts:

Intent block, Kit T2, goal statement, targeted prompt.

What is:

Three cases in different contexts where changing the intent changes the entire response: (A) an email about a delivery delay — intent: "explain" vs. "retain the customer"; (B) a lesson plan on fractions — intent: "teach the concept" vs. "prepare for the test"; (C) a contract analysis — intent: "list clauses" vs. "identify risks for the client".

Why learn:

Concrete cases from different fields prove that the principle isn’t specific to marketing—it’s universal. Anyone who recognizes the pattern can apply it in any professional situation.

Key concepts:

Universality of intent, contextual intent, cross-cutting cases, cross-functional standard.

What is:

A 5-item mental checklist to run through before any important request: (1) Do I know what real effect I want? (2) Did I state that effect in the prompt? (3) Is the audience defined? (4) Does the intent conflict with the task I asked for? (5) If the AI does exactly what I asked, will I be happy?

Why learn:

Habits are built with triggers. The checklist is the trigger — something concrete and quick that turns intention from a concept into daily practice. Item 5 is the most powerful: it forces you to anticipate the outcome before you ask.

Key concepts:

Intent checklist, habit trigger, anticipating the result, the 5 items.

2.2 ~45 min

🏹 Intent Applied to Results

From theory to practice: intent in content, sales, management, and customer service — plus the Latecomer Shortcut and the "In Your Profession" section with 5 real-world profiles. You’ll leave with a reusable statement of intent for your work.

What is:

Every piece of content exists to cause something: engagement, a new follower, a link click, a signup, a share, a comment, a change in beliefs. Before asking AI to create content, you define the human success metric — and state it in the prompt.

Why learn:

Creators who work with clear intent produce less and perform better. AI won’t ask, "What do you want to achieve?" — you need to know and say.

Key concepts:

Human-centered content metric, desired effect, creator intent, cause vs. format.

What is:

In sales, the intention isn’t to "inform people about the product"—it’s to "lead the reader to make a specific decision": schedule a conversation, click to buy, request a quote, download the material. AI writes radically different copy depending on which of these decisions you specify.

Why learn:

Sales copy that doesn't sell almost always has a vague intent — "show the product," "explain the benefits." The right question before writing any copy: "what's the one thing I want the reader to do after they finish reading?"

Key concepts:

Reader decision, single call to action, copy intent, clear next step.

What is:

Analyses, reports, and summaries exist to enable decisions — not just to exist. "Analyze the quarter’s sales data" produces one thing; "analyze the quarter’s data so I can decide whether to keep or cut product X" produces something completely different and much more useful.

Why learn:

Managers who master intent turn AI into a decision assistant, not a report generator. The difference is one extra phrase in the prompt — "so I can decide [X]."

Key concepts:

Enabled decision-making, analysis with intent, useful report vs. complete report, AI as a decision-making copilot.

What is:

In customer support, AI can "respond to the complaint" (task) or "leave the customer more satisfied after the interaction than before" (intention). The second instruction generates empathy, a sense of urgency, a concrete solution, and follow-up—not just a polite response.

Why learn:

Mediocre customer service happens when the intent is "close the ticket." Memorable service happens when the intent is "turn a frustrated customer into a brand advocate." AI handles both equally well — you choose which one.

Key concepts:

Service intent, customer emotional state, resolve vs. respond, brand advocate.

What is:

Tone is the packaging for intent. Sales intent calls for urgency, clarity, and a next step. Authority-building intent calls for depth and trust. Engagement intent calls for provocation and openness to dialogue. Stating the intent includes stating the tone that serves it.

Why learn:

The most common conflict: a sales intent with an academic tone. The copy explains everything correctly but prompts no action—because the tone signaled "learn" when the intent was "act now."

Key concepts:

Tone as packaging, tone/intent alignment, style/objective conflict, tone stated in the prompt.

What is:

No time for the complete T2 Kit? One sentence at the end of any prompt transforms the result: “My real goal here is ___.” This sentence activates the full logic of intent without requiring you to restructure the request. It’s the minimum viable expression of intent—it works in 10 seconds.

Why learn:

Those who join late don’t have time to rebuild all their habits at once. A shortcut that works now is worth more than a perfect system that starts tomorrow. This sentence is the minimum habit of layer 2.

Key concepts:

Minimum viable intention, anchor phrase, entry habit, “the real goal here is.”

What is:

Intent translated into 5 professional realities: teacher (what should the student be able to do after the lesson?), lawyer (what decision should the client’s opinion support?), salesperson (what concrete next step should the lead take after reading this?), manager (what decision does this analysis enable?), creator (what should the audience do after watching/reading?).

Why learn:

Abstract principles don’t change habits—examples from your world do. This topic is where you see yourself in the mirror and understand exactly what your default intent is and where it tends to fail.

Key concepts:

Intent by profession, teacher/lawyer/salesperson/manager/creator, immediate application, see yourself in the example.
